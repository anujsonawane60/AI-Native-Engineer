## Key learnings
- Tell the champion, the decision-maker and the gatekeeper apart before you pitch
- Write a partnership down as who does what by when, with one owner per side
- Structure a pilot with an end date and a written success criterion
- Write a cold message that leads with their problem and asks for one thing
- Catch the partnership that is enthusiasm with no owner, no date and no number

## Lesson

### The pain

A team wants their lunch pre-ordering product in three colleges, so they send the same email to two hundred institutions, subject line "Revolutionary AI Platform for Campus Food Management". Zero replies. They conclude that cold outreach does not work in India, which is the wrong conclusion from one badly designed experiment.

A second team does better and still ends with nothing. The secretary of a large student society loves their product and promises to promote it to eighteen hundred members. Nothing is written down: no date, no owner, no definition of "promote". Three weeks later the secretary has exams, and the follow-up gets a friendly, vague reply — the most expensive kind. A third team agrees to build a feature to close a "partnership" with a canteen: two weeks of work, a feature nobody else wants, and the canteen still has not started. All three treated a relationship as a channel without writing down what each side does.

### The idea

**Distribution through someone else's audience works when you make their job easier, name one owner on each side, and write down what each side does by when.**

Three conditions, and all three are required. Their incentive must be real and stated in their terms, not yours. A named human on each side must be accountable, because "the society" cannot do anything and Priya can. And there must be a date, because an agreement with no date is a feeling.

### The analogy

The canteen wants its menu on the board inside the hostel mess. The owner does not ask the warden for a favour: he notices the warden gets complaints every evening about students having nothing to eat after the mess closes, and offers something that reduces them — he prints the board himself, delivers it, and puts his own number on it so students call him and not the warden. They agree it runs for one month, then they look again. He led with the warden's problem, removed work rather than adding it, and put an end date on it so saying yes was cheap.

**Where the analogy breaks:** the warden decides alone and an institution rarely does. There is usually a **champion** who loves your product and has no authority, a **decision-maker** who can say yes and has never heard of you, and a **gatekeeper** — an office, an administrator, a compliance person — who cannot say yes but can absolutely say no. One conversation becomes three, with three different arguments.

### Picture it

```
  WHO YOU ARE ACTUALLY TALKING TO

  CHAMPION          has the problem, loves your product,
                    cannot approve anything
                    ──▶ give them the words and the proof
                        to take upward

  DECISION-MAKER    can say yes, has not heard of you,
                    cares about risk and about their own
                    priorities
                    ──▶ give them a small, reversible yes

  GATEKEEPER        cannot say yes, can say no
                    data, money, policy, procurement
                    ──▶ ask them early what would block it

  the usual failure: six weeks of delighting the champion,
  then discovering nobody asked the gatekeeper
```

Three people with three different concerns, and a pitch aimed only at the enthusiastic one stalls before it reaches anyone who can approve it.

A cold message is short, specific, and asks for one thing.

| Line | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Why them | Something you observed about their situation that you could not have written to anybody else — this is the whole message |
| What you do | Their outcome in their terms, not your category |
| Proof | A number, however small: "sixty-one students used it twice last week" |
| One ask | Fifteen minutes this week, or a reply to one question — never "let me know if you are interested" |
| An easy exit | "If this is not relevant, no reply needed", which raises reply rates by removing the cost of saying no |

Send twenty of these, personally, to people you chose one at a time. Twenty messages that each name something specific outperform two hundred that do not. Keep a sheet — who, when, what you said, what came back — because most replies come from the follow-up and you will not remember.

### Where it lives

Chapter 7's launch buys a spike; this chapter builds a channel that compounds slowly. Chapter 1's position is what the cold message's second line says. Chapter 5's CAC applies here too and is usually ugly at first, because institutional outreach consumes founder hours — compute it anyway, or you will keep a channel that costs ₹900 a customer out of sentiment. M01's reachability test is the filter: you can reach the societies and canteens at your own college, and you cannot reach two hundred colleges by email. Chapter 10 answers the data and contract questions; chapter 11 reviews whether any of this works.

### Words to own

| Term | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Warm intro | An introduction from someone they trust | A senior who knows them |
| Cold outreach | Contacting someone with no connection | Twenty chosen emails |
| Champion | Loves it, cannot approve it | The society secretary |
| Decision-maker | Can say yes | The head of department |
| Gatekeeper | Can say no | The admin office |
| Pilot | A scoped, time-boxed trial | One hostel, four weeks |
| Success criterion | The agreed test of whether it worked | 40 orders in week 4 |
| Reply rate | Replies divided by messages sent | 4 of 20 |
| Give-first | Being useful before promoting | Answering questions |
| Scope creep | New requirements arriving mid-pilot | A custom feature |
| MoU | A written statement of what each side does | One page, signed |

### Tradeoffs and gotchas

**With an institution, the unit of progress is a pilot.** Scope — one hostel, not the campus. An end date, four weeks, after which something is decided. A success criterion in their terms, agreed before it starts: "at least forty students place an order in week four". And a named owner on each side. A pilot with no end date is a free product with an audience of one; one with no success criterion ends with both sides holding different opinions, and the side holding the budget wins.

**Do not promise features to close a pilot.** One institution's special requirement is two weeks of work and a product that fits nobody else. Say "not now, and here is what we will do instead". Be careful too about who owns the data, because the moment an institution's records are involved, chapter 10's questions arrive.

| Gotcha | What to do about it |
|---|---|
| Participate before you promote | Communities have self-promotion rules and a culture that notices extraction — be useful first, so your post lands as a member sharing work |
| Enthusiasm is not a commitment | "We would love to work together" starts a conversation; answer with a small step and a date, then send two lines recording what each side does by when |
| Partnerships are slow | Run them alongside direct acquisition, never instead of it |
| A partner who wants exclusivity | They are asking you to shrink your market in exchange for a promise; at your stage, politely no |
| Revenue sharing | Needs a number before it needs a contract: what percentage, of what, paid when, measured by whom |
| One owner per side, or it dies | Shared responsibility across a committee means nobody follows up and the partnership expires silently |

### Verify it

**Red flag, the headline one: a partnership with no named owner and no date.** Ask who on their side is accountable and what happens by when. If either answer is a group or "soon", you do not have a partnership.

**Red flag: a pilot with no end date or no success criterion.** Both, in writing, before it starts. Without them a pilot runs forever and is judged by whoever is in the room at the end.

**Red flag: outreach that leads with your product.** Check the first line. If it describes you rather than them, it is a broadcast with a greeting on it.

**Red flag: a template with a merge field and nothing specific.** AI will happily generate two hundred personalised-looking emails, but what earns a reply is the observation that could only have been written to that person — the one thing it cannot invent for you.

**Red flag: a plan that only ever talks to the champion.** Ask who can approve this and who could block it. If nobody has asked the gatekeeper what would stop it, the answer arrives in week six.

**Red flag: a feature promised to close a deal, or community promotion with no history of participation.** Count the users who want that feature besides this partner, and check your own posting record before you post.

## Walkthrough
1. List every institution, society, club, shop or business you can reach *by name through someone you know*. Warm beats cold by a wide margin, so exhaust this list first.
2. For your top three targets, write down the three people separately: who has the problem, who can approve it, and who could block it. If you cannot name the decision-maker, that is your first research task.
3. For each target, write one sentence on what *they* get, in their own terms. If it is a benefit to you dressed as a benefit to them, rewrite it.
4. Write one cold message in the five-line structure. Write the first line last, and make it something you could not have sent to anyone else.
5. Send twenty messages, chosen one at a time, over two days. Record each one in a sheet: who, when, what you said.
6. Follow up once, after four working days, with one new piece of information rather than a reminder.
7. Pick a community you genuinely belong to and spend two weeks being useful in it without mentioning your product once. Record what you contributed.
8. For your first interested partner, write a one-page pilot: scope, start date, end date, success criterion in their words, and one named owner on each side. Send it and ask them to confirm by reply.
9. Ask the gatekeeper directly what would block this — data, money, policy, permission — before you build anything for the pilot.
10. At the end of the pilot, measure the success criterion exactly as written, and compute CAC for this channel including every hour your team spent. Then decide whether to repeat it.

## Talk to AI
```prompt
I need to get my product into institutions and communities, and I want you to help me prepare rather than write me templates.

My product and position, one sentence: [from chapter 1]
My target: [the specific institution, society, canteen, shop or business]
What I know about them, specifically: [anything observed - complaints they get, how they do this today, who runs it]
People I can reach there by name, and how I know them: [list, or "nobody"]
What I think they get from this: [in their terms]
Proof I actually have: [numbers, however small, or "none yet"]
Communities I am a genuine member of: [list, with what I have contributed]
What one hour of my team's time is worth: [from chapter 5]

Do all of this, in order:

1. For my target, tell me who the likely champion, decision-maker and gatekeeper are, given what I described. If I cannot name one of them, tell me what to go and find out and how.

2. Tell me what the gatekeeper will most likely raise - data, money, policy, permission - and what I should ask them before I build anything.

3. Rewrite my "what they get" sentence in their terms. If my version is a benefit to me wearing their clothes, say so.

4. Help me write the five-line cold message: why them specifically, what I do in their terms, one piece of proof, one small ask, and an easy exit. For the first line, do NOT invent an observation - tell me exactly what I would need to find out in order to write it truthfully.

5. Tell me what reply rate I should expect from twenty well-chosen messages, what I should conclude from zero replies, and what follow-up actually works.

6. Draft my one-page pilot structure: scope, dates, a success criterion written in their words that we would both measure the same way, and one named owner per side. Leave the criterion for me to agree with them rather than inventing it.

7. Tell me the three ways this pilot most likely goes wrong, and what sentence I should have ready for each one - especially the request for a custom feature.

8. Tell me how to compute CAC for this channel including my own hours, and at what number I should stop doing institutional outreach altogether.

9. Finally, review my community list. Tell me which ones I would burn by posting promotionally today, and what give-first participation would look like in each.
```

## Quiz

### Q1 · L3
A society secretary loves your product and promises to promote it to 1,800 members. Nothing happens. What was missing?
- (a) A written contract with legal terms
- (b) A named owner on each side, a definition of "promote", and a date ✅
- (c) A revenue-sharing agreement
- (d) A larger audience than 1,800 members
> Enthusiasm with no owner, no scope and no date is a feeling. A two-line confirmation naming who does what by when is enough, and it is the step teams skip because it feels unnecessary.

### Q2 · L4
You have spent six weeks delighting an enthusiastic contact at a college and the deal has not moved. What is the most likely explanation?
- (a) Your product is not good enough yet
- (b) They are a champion who cannot approve anything, and nobody has spoken to the decision-maker or the gatekeeper ✅
- (c) Institutions in India do not adopt student products
- (d) Your price is too high for an institution
> A champion has the problem and no authority. Your job is to give them the words and proof to take upward, and to ask the gatekeeper early what would block it.

### Q3 · L4
Which pilot structure is most likely to end in a decision rather than drift?
- (a) Free access for the whole campus until they see the value
- (b) One hostel, four weeks, with an agreed success criterion of forty orders in week four and one owner per side ✅
- (c) A three-month trial with a review whenever they are ready
- (d) Full access in exchange for a testimonial
> Scope, an end date, a criterion in their words agreed in advance, and a named owner each. Without the date and the criterion, a pilot is a free product judged by whoever is in the room at the end.

### Q4 · L3
Why does a cold message's first line matter more than everything after it?
- (a) It determines whether the email reaches the inbox
- (b) It is the only part that proves this message could not have been sent to anybody else ✅
- (c) It establishes your credibility as a founder
- (d) It sets up the ask that follows
> Specificity is what earns a reply. Everything after the first line is logistics, and a generated line with a merge field in it signals a broadcast however fluent it reads.

### Q5 · L5
An AI offers to generate two hundred personalised outreach emails for your institutional targets. What is the flaw?
- (a) The messages will be too long
- (b) It cannot supply the one observation about each target that makes the message worth replying to ✅
- (c) It will use the wrong tone for Indian institutions
- (d) Bulk sending will damage your domain reputation
> Personalised-looking is not personal. The observation has to come from your own research into that one target, which is exactly why twenty chosen messages beat two hundred generated ones.
