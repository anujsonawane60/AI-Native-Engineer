## Key learnings
- Write a position a stranger can repeat to a friend sixty seconds later
- Position against the workaround your users have today, not against a competitor
- Name the four parts: who it is for, what they do today, what changes, why you
- Tell a position apart from a tagline, a feature list and a category claim
- Spot the AI-written position that would fit three other products unchanged

## Lesson

### The pain

Three teams present on the same Thursday. Team A is building "an AI-powered platform for campus services". Team B is building "a smarter way to manage your college life". Team C says: "second-year students whose labs run eleven to one order lunch by 10:45 and collect it at a side window, instead of standing in the twenty-minute canteen queue." Two weeks later A and B have four hundred visits between them and three signups. Team C has sixty-one users.

A's product worked and B's was prettier. What both lacked was a sentence a student could hear once in a corridor and repeat accurately in class — so the next person could decide in two seconds whether it was for them. Teams skip this because it produces no code, then conclude they need more features.

### The idea

**Positioning is the sentence someone else repeats when you are not in the room, and it is built from four facts, not from adjectives.**

Who exactly this is for, what they do about the problem today, what specifically changes for them, and why you rather than the obvious alternative. The logo, the tagline and the hero image only decorate that. Your technology is not on the list: "AI-powered" is a fact about your implementation, and users do not buy implementations.

The "today" fact is the one teams get wrong. Your real competition is the workaround from M01 — a WhatsApp group, a friend who queues, or doing nothing — almost never a startup. It also hands you your number: twenty minutes is in Team C's position because the queue costs twenty minutes. A position with no number in it is usually an adjective in disguise.

### The analogy

Two canteens, forty metres apart. The first board says "FOOD · SNACKS · BEVERAGES" — accurate and useless, because everything on campus is food, snacks or beverages. The second says "FULL THALI · ₹60 · SERVED IN 7 MINUTES · 1 TO 2 PM". A student with forty minutes free and ₹60 has already decided, and tells a friend "the place near the gate does a thali in seven minutes" — a sentence that survives repetition because it is made of facts, and that is aimed at something specific: the mess queue, not restaurants in town.

**Where the analogy breaks:** a canteen is positioned partly by where it physically stands, and a student walking past discovers it with no words at all. Your product has no gate and no smell. Words, and only words, decide whether anyone finds out it exists — which is why eight unglamorous minutes on four lines is the highest-return work in this module.

### Picture it

```
  THE FOUR LINES, filled in

  FOR        second-year students with 11-1 labs, Nashik hostels
  TODAY      they send a friend to queue, or they skip lunch
  WE         take the order by 10:45, hand it over at a window
  UNLIKE     the canteen queue, which costs them 20 minutes
  PROOF      61 students, 2 orders each, last week

  THE SAME FOUR LINES, unfilled
  FOR        students
  TODAY      -
  WE         provide a smart platform for campus life
  UNLIKE     other apps
  PROOF      -
```

Two versions of the same template: the filled one names people, a workaround, a specific change and evidence, while the unfilled one could describe any product in the cohort.

### Where it lives

Your position is an input to nearly everything that follows: chapter 2's price depends on which workaround you displace and what it costs, chapter 3's model on who the user is and how often value arrives, and chapters 7 and 8 are only this sentence distributed — loudly, or to one person at a time. Backwards it rests on M01; if the position feels impossible to write, the usual cause is a problem statement that was never narrowed, not a lack of copywriting skill. M03 gets it onto a page. M18 wants a second version for a jury, which asks about market and insight — write the user's version first.

### Words to own

| Term | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Position | The repeatable sentence about who and why | Team C's four lines |
| Tagline | A short memorable phrase; decoration only | "Lunch, sorted" |
| Category | The familiar box you ask to be filed under | Lunch pre-ordering |
| Alternative | What they genuinely use today | Sending a friend to queue |
| Differentiator | The one thing you do that the alternative cannot | Guaranteed 10:45 cutoff |
| ICP | Ideal customer profile; the narrow who | 2nd-year, 11-1 labs |
| Proof point | Evidence a stranger would accept | 61 users, 2 orders each |
| Switching cost | What it costs them to change | Learning a new app |
| Value proposition | What changes for them, in their words | 20 minutes back, daily |

### Tradeoffs and gotchas

**Narrowing feels like shrinking the market, and it is the opposite.** "Second-year students with 11-1 labs" is a hundred people you can reach, convince and watch. "All students" is a number you cannot act on. You widen later from a base.

**Do not invent a category.** "We are building the Campus OS" asks a listener to learn a new concept before deciding anything. Borrow a category they already understand and narrow it hard: "lunch pre-ordering for hostel students" needs no explanation. Boring and understood beats novel and ignored.

**"AI-powered" is not a position.** If the AI genuinely is the differentiator, say what it does for the user: "it reads your timetable and orders without you opening the app" is a position; "AI-powered" is a shrug.

**A position you cannot deliver is the most expensive kind.** If the board says seven minutes and it takes eighteen, you have mis-sold, and the user who tried you tells everyone — chapter 9 is how fast that spreads. Repositioning is expensive too: the sentence is on a landing page, a store listing, two hundred emails and your pitch.

### Verify it

AI fails here in one recognisable way: it writes fluent positioning that is interchangeable.

**Red flag: the swap test fails.** Put a competitor's name in place of yours and read it again. If it still reads true, it is a mood, not a position. This catches almost every first draft, human or machine.

**Red flag: no named alternative.** If the sentence does not say what the user stops doing, nobody can judge whether to switch. "Instead of" is load-bearing.

**Red flag: adjectives where a number belongs.** Seamless, smart, effortless, modern, powerful — each marks a place where a number was unavailable. Twenty minutes, ₹60 and 10:45 survive repetition.

**Red flag: a position written before anyone was asked.** The model will produce a confident position from your feature list alone, and it will be about your features. Your five M01 conversations are the only source for what the user calls the problem.

Then the test no desk work replaces: say your sentence to five people who do not know your project, change the subject for a minute, and ask them what it was. What comes back is your actual position.

## Walkthrough
1. Open your M01 problem statement and your notes from the willingness-to-pay conversations. Underline every phrase where a real user described the problem in their own words. You are going to steal those words.
2. Write the FOR line as narrowly as you can bear: a year, a place, a situation, a time of day. If it does not make you slightly uncomfortable, narrow it again.
3. Write the TODAY line: exactly what these people do about this problem right now, including "nothing". Name the workaround, not a competitor product.
4. Put a number on the TODAY line — minutes, rupees, trips, messages. If you do not have one, write down the question you need to ask to get it.
5. Write the WE line as what changes for the user, with no mention of your stack, your model or your framework.
6. Write the UNLIKE line against the workaround from step 3, naming the one thing it cannot do that you can.
7. Write the PROOF line with whatever evidence you honestly have today, even if it is "nine people tried it and seven came back". If you have none, write "none yet" rather than inventing one.
8. Run the swap test: put a rival name in place of yours and read all five lines aloud. Rewrite every line that still reads true.
9. Say the sentence to five people outside your team, wait a minute, and ask them to repeat it back. Write down what each one actually said.
10. Rewrite the position using the words that came back, and commit it to your repository as `POSITIONING.md`. Chapters 2, 7 and 8 all read from this file.

## Talk to AI
```prompt
I am writing the positioning for my startup and I want you to attack it, not polish it. Do not write marketing copy for me.

What we built: [one or two sentences, plainly]
Who I think it is for: [be as narrow as you can]
What those people do about this problem today, in their own words: [the workaround, with a number if you have one]
What I believe changes for them: [one sentence]
Evidence I actually have: [users, repeat usage, or "none yet"]
Direct quotes from real users, if I have any: [paste them]

Now do all of this, in order:

1. Restate my position in one sentence as a stranger would understand it. If you cannot, tell me which of my inputs was too vague to use.

2. Run the swap test on it. Name two other products - real or plausible - that my sentence would also describe truthfully. If you find any, tell me my position is a mood rather than a position.

3. List every adjective I used and, for each one, tell me what number or fact would have to replace it to make the claim testable.

4. Tell me whether I am positioning against the real workaround or against a competitor my users have probably never used. Quote the line that gives it away.

5. Give me three narrower versions of my FOR line, each excluding people I currently include, and tell me what each one gains and loses.

6. Write the four lines - FOR, TODAY, WE, UNLIKE - using only words that appear in my user quotes. If my quotes do not contain enough material, say so and tell me the three questions to go and ask.

7. Tell me the one claim in my position I am most likely to fail to deliver, and what happens to a user who believes it and does not get it.

Be blunt. Do not tell me it is compelling. Tell me where a stranger stops caring.
```

## Stuck? Ask AI
```prompt
I keep writing positioning that sounds like every other student project. Here is my latest attempt: [paste it]. Do not rewrite it. Instead, go line by line and mark each phrase as either a fact, an adjective, or a claim about my technology. Then tell me how much is left once the adjectives and technology claims are removed.
```

```prompt
Explain the difference between a position, a tagline, a category and a value proposition, using one real Indian consumer product as the example for all four. Then give me five short statements and ask me to sort them into those four buckets, without telling me the answers first.
```

```prompt
My team cannot agree on who our product is for. Here are the three groups we keep arguing about: [list them, with what each group does about the problem today]. Do not pick for me. For each group, tell me what we would have to build, where we would have to go to find them, and what we would be giving up by choosing them.
```

## Quiz

### Q1 · L3
Which of these is a position rather than a tagline or a feature claim?
- (a) The smartest way to manage your campus life
- (b) AI-powered scheduling built on a modern stack
- (c) Second-year students with 11-1 labs order lunch by 10:45 and collect it at a window, instead of queueing twenty minutes ✅
- (d) Lunch, sorted
> A position names who it is for, what they do today, and what changes, with facts a listener can judge. The other three are decoration, implementation detail, or a mood.

### Q2 · L4
You replace your product's name with a competitor's in your positioning sentence and it still reads as true. What does that tell you?
- (a) The market is crowded, so you need more features
- (b) Your sentence describes a category, not your product, so it cannot help anyone choose ✅
- (c) Your competitor has copied your positioning
- (d) Nothing; all positioning in a category sounds similar
> The swap test is the cheapest check there is. If the sentence survives the swap, it contains nothing that distinguishes you, and a user reading it has no basis for a decision.

### Q3 · L4
Your users currently solve the problem by sending a friend to stand in the queue. Why should your UNLIKE line be about that rather than about a rival app?
- (a) Rival apps are usually better funded, so comparison is unwise
- (b) Because the workaround is what they would actually be giving up, and it is what they can judge you against ✅
- (c) Because mentioning competitors is legally risky
- (d) Because the workaround is cheaper than any app
> You are asking someone to change a habit, not to switch products. The thing they must stop doing is the only honest basis for comparison, and it is usually not software at all.

### Q4 · L3
Why is "AI-powered" a weak position for a 2026 consumer product in India?
- (a) Users do not believe AI claims
- (b) It describes your implementation rather than what changes in the user's day ✅
- (c) It attracts regulatory attention under the DPDP Act
- (d) It makes the product sound expensive
> Users buy outcomes, not architectures. "Reads your timetable and orders without you opening the app" is a position; the phrase "AI-powered" is a fact about you.

### Q5 · L5
An AI writes you a fluent, confident position from your feature list alone. What is the most likely defect in it?
- (a) It will be too short to be useful
- (b) It will describe your features in your words, and be interchangeable with other products ✅
- (c) It will exaggerate your traction
- (d) It will pick the wrong category
> With no user language to work from, the model fills the gap with generic fluency. The repair is not better prompting; it is the five conversations from M01, used as the source material.
