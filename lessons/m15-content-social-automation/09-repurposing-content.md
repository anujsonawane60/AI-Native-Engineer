## Key learnings
- Turn one arguable claim into ten format-native pieces without changing the claim
- Rewrite the hook for every format instead of reusing one first line
- Treat a Hindi or Marathi version as its own piece, not a translation
- Give every derivative a canonical home on a page you own
- Spot repurposing that is only the same text pasted into a different box

## Lesson

### The pain

A team finds something genuinely good: of their 1,240 orders, 61% were placed between 8:05 pm and 8:40 pm, and they can argue from it that evening-slot capacity, not menu variety, decides whether a tiffin service survives. Nobody else has that number. They post it once — one caption, 41 followers, 11 pm — and nine people see it. A second team fares no better by "repurposing", which for them means pasting the same 400 words into five boxes: the short version truncates mid-sentence, the caption opens "In today's competitive food delivery landscape", and the WhatsApp message runs eleven lines, so nobody forwards it — and forwarding was the only reason to be on WhatsApp. One idea published once is waste; one idea pasted five times is also waste, and slightly embarrassing.

### The idea

**One idea is a source, not a post. Repurposing means re-deciding the hook, the length, the format and sometimes the language, while the claim itself stays exactly the same.**

If the claim changes between formats you do not have one idea in ten places, you have ten half-ideas and no position. If only the box changes, you have copied. The work is in the middle.

| Format | What it is | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Article on your own site | The canonical version, 400–800 words | Full argument, the evidence table |
| Vertical video, 30–60s | One number, one claim, burned-in captions | Five-second hook, no intro |
| Longer video, 3–6 min | The argument with the data on screen | Spoken, chapters, searchable title |
| Carousel, 6 cards | Card 1 the number, card 6 the claim | One idea per card, no paragraphs |
| Text thread | First line standing alone, then the steps | Each part readable on its own |
| WhatsApp forward | Two lines, one image, one link | Written so somebody forwards it |
| FAQ or glossary page | Answers one exact query from chapter 2 | Query as the heading, answer first |
| Regional-language version | Its own piece in Marathi or Hindi | Written, not translated |
| One-screen image | The number as the whole design | Chapter 7's kit, three ratios |
| Direct message to 40 people | Personal, one line, no broadcast feel | Named, specific, asks a question |

The hook is the part you rewrite, and it is where most repurposing fails. Search wants the query itself: *"How much does a monthly tiffin cost in Nashik?"* A feed wants curiosity inside five words, before the "more" link cuts it off: *"61% of our orders land in 35 minutes."* WhatsApp wants a reason to forward, which is almost always usefulness to a third person: *"Useful if you run a tiffin service — their busiest 35 minutes, with numbers."* A video wants a reason not to swipe within two seconds. Five hooks, one claim: twenty minutes of work, and the difference between nine views and nine hundred.

### The analogy

One pot of usal, cooked at six in the morning. It becomes misal with tarri on top, usal-pav wrapped for the walk to class, one compartment of the thali, and tomorrow's gravy base — one thing, prepared each time for how it will actually be eaten: misal in a bowl with a spoon, usal-pav in paper, in one hand.

**Where the analogy breaks** in two ways. The pot is finite, which forces the cook to choose; an idea is infinitely copyable, so your limit is not supply but your audience's patience. And nobody minds eating misal twice, while a reader who sees identical words twice unfollows. Reuse the substance, never the sentences.

### Picture it

```
        ONE CLAIM - one sentence somebody could argue with
                              │
   ┌──────────┬───────────────┼───────────┬────────────┐
   ▼          ▼               ▼           ▼            ▼
 article   60s vertical   6-card      WhatsApp     FAQ page
 on your   video           carousel    forward      for one
 own site                              2 lines      query
   │          │               │           │            │
   ▼          ▼               ▼           ▼            ▼
 new hook, new length, new format, sometimes new language
          every derivative links back to the article
```

A single claim fans out into formats that differ in hook, length, shape and language, while the claim stays identical and every derivative points back to one page you own.

### Where it lives

Chapter 5 produces the claim and the evidence; this chapter multiplies it. Chapter 6 keeps the voice constant while the format changes, chapter 7 supplies the imagery and ratios, chapter 8 carries the variants to each channel.

Order matters: publish the article on your own site **first**, and point every derivative at it. The page is the only version you control and the only one that can be indexed, so chapters 2 to 4 need it to exist; the link gives every social post somewhere to send the people it earns; and when a platform's reach collapses, the thing you own survives.

### Words to own

| Term | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Source piece | The canonical full version you own | The article on your site |
| Derivative | A format made from the source | The carousel, the reel |
| Atomic idea | One claim small enough to repeat | The 8:40 pm finding |
| Format-native | Built for how that format is consumed | Captions burned in, muted |
| Hook | The first line or two seconds | "61% in 35 minutes" |
| Canonical home | The page every derivative links to | Your own article |
| Forwardable | Short and useful to a third person | Two lines and a link |
| Repurposing | Re-deciding format around one claim | Ten pieces, one idea |
| Reposting | Publishing the same thing again later | Fine, when the audience changed |
| Cadence | How often and how spread out | Ten pieces over two weeks |
| Evergreen | Still true in six months | A price comparison page |

### Tradeoffs and gotchas

**Do not repurpose a bad idea.** Ten formats of a claim nobody could disagree with is ten times nothing. Chapter 5's disagreement test comes first.

**Pick four formats, not ten, in your first month.** Video costs roughly ten times a text post, so choose the four your audience actually consumes — from your chapter 1 interviews, not from a list.

**Never let the claim drift.** If the article says capacity matters more than variety and the reel says variety matters, you have published a contradiction.

**Language is a format, not a translation.** A Marathi version is its own piece: translating word for word produces something grammatical that no Marathi speaker would say, and your audience hears it immediately. Write it for how people actually talk, with the named person from chapter 6's language policy reading it aloud first. It is also your least contested distribution — almost nobody is writing well in Marathi about your subject.

**Stagger, do not dump.** Ten pieces in one day reaches the same small group ten times. And reposting is legitimate: your audience in week ten is not the audience of week two.

**Timely ideas expire; evergreen ones compound.** A festival post is dead in a week; a price-comparison page works for two years.

### Verify it

**Red flag: the same text in a different box.** Put the article and the carousel side by side. If the carousel's words appear verbatim in the article, no decision was made about the format.

**Red flag: identical first lines.** Line up the opening line of every piece. More than two matching means the hooks were not rewritten, and the hook is the whole job.

**Red flag: a thread whose first line does not stand alone.** Read part one with no context. If it needs part two to make sense, nobody reaches part two.

**Red flag: a WhatsApp message longer than three lines.** Would you forward this to one person? If not, nobody else will either.

**Red flag: a translated version nobody read aloud.** Ask the named person in your language policy to read it to you. If they wince once, it is not ready.

**Red flag: the claim changed.** Check each derivative against the one sentence at the top of your document. Drift is a defect, not a variation.

**Red flag: no canonical home.** If no derivative links back to a page you own, you have grown someone else's platform.

**Red flag: a format with no evidence left in it.** Count the numbers and proper nouns in each piece. A derivative that lost the evidence is filler — chapter 5's failure in new clothes.

## Walkthrough
1. Take your single best claim from chapter 5 and write it as one sentence at the top of a fresh document. Everything in this walkthrough gets checked against that sentence.
2. Write the evidence under it: the number, the quote, the failure. Every derivative must keep at least one of these.
3. From your chapter 1 interviews, list the formats your actual users consume. Rank them. Cross out everything below the top four.
4. Write the canonical article first, on your own site, with the query from chapter 2 as the heading. Publish it before anything else.
5. Write one hook per format — at least four, each under fifteen words. Read them side by side and rewrite any that resemble another.
6. Build the four derivatives. For each, change length, shape and hook, and keep the claim and at least one piece of evidence identical.
7. Write the WhatsApp version last and hold it to two lines plus one link. Send it to one real person and see whether they forward it.
8. Produce the regional-language version as its own piece. Have the named person from your language policy read it aloud to you before it ships.
9. Make sure every derivative links back to the canonical article, then schedule them staggered across two weeks using chapter 8's queue, one row per channel.
10. After two weeks, record which format produced the most clicks and the most replies, and write one sentence on whether that matched what you expected.

## Talk to AI
```prompt
CONTEXT:      I am a student founder. I do not write code; I direct AI.
              My product: [one sentence]. My audience: [who, where,
              languages, what phone].

              MY ONE CLAIM, unchanged in every format:
              [one sentence somebody could disagree with]

              MY EVIDENCE - use only these, add nothing:
              - [number from my own data, with what it counts]
              - [a real user quote in their words]
              - [something my team got wrong]

              My canonical article, already published: [paste it or its
              URL]
              My voice rules and bans from VOICE.md: [paste them]
              Formats my users actually consume, in order: [list four]

GOAL:         Four format-native derivatives of this one claim, each
              with its own hook, plus a Marathi or Hindi version
              written for how people actually speak.

REQUIREMENTS: 1. Keep my claim identical in every piece, word for word
                 where it is stated
              2. Every piece carries at least one item of my evidence
              3. A different hook for each format, each under 15 words,
                 and say why that hook suits that format
              4. Respect each format's real shape: caption length,
                 cards, thread parts, a two-line WhatsApp message, and
                 a video script hooked in the first two seconds
              5. Every piece links back to my canonical article
              6. The regional-language version must be written, not
                 translated, and you must flag any line you are unsure a
                 native speaker would actually say

CONSTRAINTS:  Free tools only. My voice bans apply to every piece. The
              WhatsApp version must be at most two lines plus one link.
              Captions for video must be written out, because viewers
              watch muted.

NON-GOALS:    Do not change my claim. Do not add statistics, examples or
              product features I did not give you. Do not produce ten
              formats - exactly four plus the language version. Do not
              reuse the same first line twice.

ACCEPTANCE:   - My claim appears unchanged in all five pieces
              - No two pieces share a first line
              - Every piece contains at least one of my three evidence
                items
              - Zero facts that are not in my evidence
              - A table showing format, hook, length and which evidence
                item it uses
              - A section titled "Where this drifted", naming any point
                where a format tempted you to soften or change my claim

ASK:          First, tell me which of my four formats is the worst fit
              for this particular claim, and why. Then produce the rest.
```

## Quiz

### Q1 · L3
What distinguishes repurposing from copying?
- (a) Repurposing uses different images
- (b) The claim and evidence stay identical while hook, length, format and sometimes language are re-decided ✅
- (c) Repurposing publishes on more platforms
- (d) Repurposing rewrites the claim for each audience
> Only the box changing is copying; the claim changing means you have ten half-ideas instead of one. The work is in the hook, the length and the shape.

### Q2 · L4
Your WhatsApp version of a post is eleven lines long. Why does that defeat the purpose?
- (a) WhatsApp truncates long messages automatically
- (b) Nobody forwards eleven lines, and forwarding was the only reason to use the channel ✅
- (c) Long messages cannot include links
- (d) WhatsApp ranks shorter messages higher
> A forward happens when the message is useful to a third person and short enough to pass on. Two lines, one image and one link is the format; anything longer is a post in the wrong place.

### Q3 · L3
Why should the canonical article on your own site be published first?
- (a) Search engines penalise social posts published before an article
- (b) It is the only version you control and can get indexed, and it gives every derivative somewhere to send people ✅
- (c) It is the cheapest format to produce
- (d) Platforms require a source link on every post
> Owning the canonical home is what makes chapters 2 to 4 possible and what survives a platform's reach collapsing. Derivatives earn attention; the page you own keeps it.

### Q4 · L4
Your article argues that capacity matters more than variety. The reel you make says variety is what customers want. What is the defect?
- (a) Nothing — different platforms need different messages
- (b) The claim drifted, so you have published a contradiction instead of one idea in two places ✅
- (c) Reels should not carry claims
- (d) The article should be updated to match the reel
> Write the claim once at the top of your working document and check every derivative against that sentence. Drift is a defect, not an adaptation.

### Q5 · L5
Your team has one strong claim and time for either ten formats of it or ten separate weak ideas. Which is better, and what decides it?
- (a) Ten separate ideas, because variety reaches more people
- (b) Ten formats of the strong claim, because the claim is what earns attention and the format only delivers it ✅
- (c) Neither; publish nothing until you have more data
- (d) Ten formats, but only if each one changes the claim slightly for its audience
> Repurposing multiplies whatever it is given. Applied to something arguable and evidenced it compounds; applied to ten claims nobody could disagree with it multiplies nothing, which is chapter 5's lesson again.
