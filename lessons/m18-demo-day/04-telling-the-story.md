## Key learnings
- Tell your product in four beats: problem, insight, product, traction
- Lead with a number from your own users, never with a market size
- State the denominator beside every number you quote
- Show one path through the product instead of touring every feature
- Reproduce every traction number live from your own dashboard

## Lesson

### The pain

Three students, six minutes each.

The first gives a feature tour — login, a menu, filters, dark mode, an AI suggestion, a referral code, a profile page — twelve features with no number in any. The panel learns what the product contains and nothing about who uses it.

The second spends two minutes on the market — tens of millions of college students, a food market worth thousands of crores. Then: how many people used the product last week? "Around fifty, I think." That undoes both minutes: the enormous numbers were decoration over an unexamined small one.

The third says: *Between 1 and 1.40 pm about a hundred and eighty students walk past our canteen. Sixty-two queue, and roughly twenty leave without buying, because from the back you cannot see what is left. We built one screen showing what is available now. Ninety-four of the two hundred and ten students we invited have used it, forty-one came back in week two, and what changed the product is that price was never the blocker — not knowing what was left was.*

A problem with a number, an insight, a product, traction with its denominator.

### The idea

**Four beats, in this order: the problem with a number in it, the insight an outsider would not guess, the product shown as one path, and traction with its denominator. Anything else is a feature tour.**

The insight beat is usually missing and the only one that cannot be faked, because it comes from watching real people use the thing. It is also what makes the other three believable.

### The analogy

The canteen owner is talking to someone who might rent them a bigger space. The weak version: "we serve fourteen dishes and the poha is very good". The strong version: *a hundred and eighty students pass between one and two, we sell sixty plates, we run out of poha by 1:20 — and what I learned is they come for speed, not variety, so I cut the menu to six and sales went up.*

**Where the analogy breaks:** a queue is self-evident, and you cannot fake one in front of someone standing in the shop. Your traction lives in a dashboard only you can open, so an inflated number costs nothing to say. That is why beat four is not spoken but **shown** — opened live, with its denominator, in front of the room.

### Picture it

```
  THE SIX-MINUTE STORY
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ 1 PROBLEM   0:00-0:45  whose problem, with a number  │
  │ 2 INSIGHT   0:45-2:00  what you learned that an      │
  │                        outsider would not guess      │
  │ 3 PRODUCT   2:00-4:30  one path, live, end to end    │
  │ 4 TRACTION  4:30-6:00  users, return rate, money -   │
  │                        each with its denominator     │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Four beats with a time budget each, where beat three is a path performed live rather than a tour of screens. Six minutes means six: overrunning steals the M18.3 debugging block.

Beat three is the one students most want to stretch and the one that must stay narrow. **One path, start to finish, the one your north star measures** — open the product as a new user would, do the thing, see the result. Then one failure state, shown rather than described.

Beat four has five rules. **Always give the denominator** — ninety-four users out of how many invited? A reviewer who has to ask has already discounted the number. **State the window**: ninety-four over six months is a different product from ninety-four in three weeks. **Separate signups from activation**, a form completed versus the thing the product exists for (M16.6). **Give the trend, not the total.** **Name what you cannot measure yet.**

```
  WEAK    "We have 94 users."
  BETTER  "94 students have used it."
  STRONG  "94 of the 210 students we invited used it over three
           weeks. 41 of those 94 came back in week two. Last
           week was 12 new and 29 returning."
```

The same fact three ways; only the third can be checked, and only the third says whether the product is working. Revenue follows the same rule: three paying users at ₹49, with your cost to serve them, is respectable. "Early monetisation traction" is not.

### Where it lives

These are the first six minutes of your defence and the only part the room takes on trust; everything after is checked. Beat one comes from M01.2–M01.3, beat two from M16.3 and M16.5, beat three from your M01.5 north star, beat four from M16's users and retention and M17's price and unit economics — the one beat that cannot be built in a week. Forward, every claim here becomes a target in M18.6.

### Words to own

| Term | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Insight | Something you learned that outsiders would not guess | "Price was never the blocker" |
| Traction | Evidence that real people use it, repeatedly | 41 of 94 returned in week two |
| Denominator | The total the number is out of | 94 of 210 invited |
| Activation | A user who did the thing the product is for | Placed an order, not just signed up |
| Retention | Users who come back in a later period | Week-two return rate |
| Cohort | A group defined by when they joined | Everyone who joined in October |
| Vanity metric | A number that only goes up and decides nothing | Total page views |
| North star | The one number your product exists to move | Orders completed per day |
| Unit economics | What one customer costs and earns you | ₹49 revenue, ₹11 to serve |
| The ask | What you want from the room, in one sentence | "Two canteens for a month's pilot" |

### Tradeoffs and gotchas

**Never open with market size.** It sets up the contrast that destroys the second student: enormous market, unknown usage.

**A number without a denominator is marketing.** This is a defence, not a pitch competition — the same number with its denominator and window is evidence, and just as impressive when small. Never round upward or invent: a figure that does not match the dashboard is the one unrecoverable error.

**An insight is not a feature.** "Users wanted a faster interface" is a feature request. "We assumed students wanted more choice; the interviews said they wanted to know what was left" is an insight: it names what you believed and what changed it.

**The story is shared; your part in it is not.** All three of you will tell the same four beats. What must differ is which user conversations you were in, which number you instrumented, which part you directed. Three identical stories from one group is the signal this module exists to catch.

### Verify it

Five checks, the first two in front of another person.

1. **Reproduce every number live, each in under sixty seconds.** One you cannot reach in a minute you will fumble in the room.
2. **Have someone ask "how do you know?" after your insight.** "It seemed obvious" is a feature request; "nine of the eleven people we watched did this" is an insight.
3. **Time the whole thing, out loud, standing up.** Reading silently takes two-thirds as long.
4. **Delete every adjective and read what is left.** If removing fast, simple and seamless leaves nothing, there was nothing.
5. **Tell it to an outsider and ask for the problem and the number back.** If they get either wrong, beat one is not working.

The red flags: traction numbers that cannot be reproduced from the dashboard; a signup count presented as usage; a total with no window; an insight that is really a feature; a feature tour instead of one completed path; and three identical stories with no contribution named.

## Walkthrough
1. Create `STORY.md` with the four beats as headings and the time budget written beside each one.
2. Write beat one in two sentences, with one number from your own observations or interviews. Not a market number — a number about the people you watched.
3. Write beat two by answering one question in writing: what did we believe in week one that turned out to be wrong, and what showed us? The answer is your insight. If nothing changed, say so honestly and name what you confirmed instead.
4. Choose beat three's single path: the one your north star measures. Write the exact sequence of taps and the one failure state you will show.
5. Open your analytics and write beat four as three numbers, each with its denominator and its window: how many used it, how many came back, and the trend against last week.
6. If you have revenue, add paying users, the amount, the period and your cost to serve, from M17.5. If you have none, write one sentence on what you would charge and what evidence you have for it.
7. Walk through every number in beats one and four and write the exact dashboard, query or screen it comes from beside it. Delete any number whose source you cannot open.
8. Strip the adjectives. Replace each one with a number or delete the sentence.
9. Record yourself telling all four beats, standing, timed, with the product open and the dashboard in a second tab.
10. Watch the recording and check two things: did you reach beat four before time ran out, and did you perform the path or describe it.
11. Tell it to someone outside your group, ask them to repeat the problem, the insight and the main number back to you, and fix whichever one they got wrong.

## Talk to AI
```prompt
I have six minutes to tell the story of a product I built, to a panel that will then question every claim I make. Interrogate my story as a sceptic who will ask for the dashboard. Do not rewrite it into a pitch, do not add enthusiasm, and do not invent any numbers.

MY STORY
Beat 1, the problem, in my own words: [paste it]
Beat 2, the insight: what I believed in week one, what changed it, and what I believe now: [paste it]
Beat 3, the single path I will show live, as a sequence of taps, plus the one failure state: [paste it]
Beat 4, my traction: [paste every number, with its denominator and its window, and say where each one comes from - which dashboard, query or screen]
Revenue, if any: [paying users, amount, period, and my cost to serve one user]
My north star metric and its value last week and the week before: [paste both]
What I personally did on this product: [which user conversations I was in, which events I instrumented, which parts I directed]

Work through all of these:

1. BEAT ONE. Does my problem statement contain a number about real people I observed, or a market number? Quote it. Could this problem statement describe twenty other products?
2. BEAT TWO. Is my insight an actual insight - something an outsider would not guess - or a feature request in disguise? Quote the part that shows what I believed before. If that part is missing, say so.
3. EVIDENCE. For my insight, write the exact question a sceptic asks next, and tell me whether what I pasted answers it.
4. BEAT THREE. Is what I described one path completed end to end, or a tour of features? Name anything I plan to show that should be cut.
5. NUMBERS. List every number I used. For each: does it have a denominator, a window, and a named source? Mark any that is a signup count being presented as usage, and any total that should be a trend.
6. HONESTY. Which of my numbers would look weaker but more credible if stated completely? Rewrite three of them in the fullest honest form, using only facts I gave you.
7. TIMING. Estimate how long my four beats take to say out loud at a normal pace. Tell me which beat to cut if I am over six minutes, and which beat I must not cut.
8. INDIVIDUAL. Based on what I said I personally did, which parts of this story am I entitled to claim as mine, and which should I attribute to a teammate?

Then write the five questions the panel is most likely to ask me after this story, hardest first, and tell me which two I currently cannot answer.

Finally: name the one number I did not give you that a reviewer will certainly ask for.
```

## Mission

**Objective.** A `STORY.md` with four beats and a time budget, every number carrying its denominator, window and source, plus a recorded six-minute telling and a screen recording that reproduces each number live from your dashboard.

**Steps.**
1. **Beat one** — the problem in two sentences, with a number about people you actually observed.
2. **Beat two** — what you believed in week one, what changed it, what you believe now.
3. **Beat three** — choose the single path your north star measures, plus one failure state to show.
4. **Beat four** — three numbers with denominators and windows, plus revenue and cost to serve if you have any.
5. **Source every number** — write the dashboard, query or screen beside it; delete anything you cannot open.
6. **Strip adjectives** — replace each with a number or cut the sentence.
7. **Record** — six minutes, standing, timed, product open, dashboard in a second tab.
8. **Reproduce live** — a screen recording getting to each number from a cold dashboard in under sixty seconds.
9. **Test on an outsider** — have them repeat the problem, insight and main number back; fix what they got wrong.

**Upload.**
- `STORY.md` → attached file; the live URL → `demo_url`
- Your six-minute recording, timed and standing → link or attachment
- A screen recording reproducing every traction number from a cold dashboard
- Your numbers table: value, denominator, window, source, for each
- Your insight, with the evidence for it: how many people, what they did
- Your revenue numbers and cost to serve, or your one sentence on what you would charge and why
- Your north star last week and the week before
- The AI interrogation conversation → share link in `ai_chat_url`
- Reflection, 150 words max → the number you were most tempted to state without its denominator, and why

**Self-check.** Tick every box before you submit.
- [ ] Beat one contains a number about people I observed, not a market number
- [ ] Beat two names what I believed before and what changed it
- [ ] My insight has evidence: a count of people and what they did
- [ ] Beat three is one path completed end to end, not a feature tour
- [ ] Beat three includes one failure state shown live, not described
- [ ] Every number in my story has a denominator
- [ ] Every number has a window, and I say it out loud
- [ ] I distinguish signups from activations wherever both exist
- [ ] At least one number is a trend against last week, not a total
- [ ] I can reach every number from a cold dashboard in under sixty seconds
- [ ] No number in my story is rounded upward or estimated
- [ ] I state at least one thing I cannot yet measure, and why
- [ ] No adjective in my story is doing a fact's job
- [ ] The whole telling fits in six minutes, timed, out loud
- [ ] An outsider repeated my problem, insight and main number back correctly
- [ ] This submission is my own work; my story names the user conversations I was in and the parts of the system I personally directed, and it differs from my teammates'

**Stretch.** Write the sixty-second version of the same story — one sentence per beat — and record it. Then write the one-sentence version. Cutting from six minutes to one forces you to find out which number is actually load-bearing, and the one-sentence version is what you will use for the rest of your working life.

## Quiz

### Q1 · L4
A student opens with the size of the Indian student market, then cannot say how many people used their product last week. Why is the opening actively harmful rather than merely weak?
- (a) Market numbers are usually inaccurate
- (b) It sets up a contrast: an enormous borrowed number beside an unexamined small one ✅
- (c) It uses time that was needed for the feature tour
- (d) Panels are not interested in market opportunity
> The borrowed number proves nothing about you and raises the obvious question. Beat one must carry a number about people you personally observed, which is the one number nobody can take from you.

### Q2 · L4
Which statement of the same fact is evidence rather than marketing?
- (a) We have strong early traction
- (b) We have 94 users
- (c) 94 students have used it so far
- (d) 94 of the 210 students we invited used it over three weeks, and 41 came back in week two ✅
> Denominator, window and a return rate. Only the last version can be checked against a dashboard, and only the last version tells you whether the product is working.

### Q3 · L5
Which of these is a genuine insight for beat two?
- (a) Users wanted a faster, simpler interface
- (b) We assumed students wanted more choice; eleven of the twelve we watched asked only what was still available ✅
- (c) We learned that students are price sensitive
- (d) We discovered that a mobile-first design was essential
> An insight names what you believed before, what contradicted it, and how many people showed you. The others are feature requests or generalities an outsider could have guessed.

### Q4 · L3
Why is beat three a single path performed live rather than a tour of your screens?
- (a) A tour takes longer than the time available
- (b) One completed path proves the product works; a tour only proves the screens exist ✅
- (c) Panels prefer to see fewer features
- (d) The failure state cannot be shown during a tour
> The path your north star measures is the product. Twelve screens in six minutes tells a room what you built and nothing about whether anyone can use it.

### Q5 · L4
All three members of a group tell the same four beats in their individual defences. What makes each one an individual submission?
- (a) Nothing — the product is shared, so the story is shared
- (b) Each names the user conversations they were in, the events they instrumented and the parts they directed ✅
- (c) Each should emphasise a different feature of the product
- (d) Each should quote a different traction number
> The product is shared; the contribution is not. Three identical stories with no individual contribution named is precisely the signal this module exists to catch.
